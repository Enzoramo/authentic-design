# Art direction from the brief

Use for fresh identities, substantial redesigns or meaningful alternatives. Preserve fixed references and existing product systems. The objective is a coherent visual language that belongs to this product, not a rotating catalog of fashionable styles.

## Separate function from visual identity

First establish the task, content and familiar controls that must work. A searchable list, a sequence and a questionnaire are different interaction models, but all can inherit the same visual shell. Do not count a change of navigation model as sufficient exploration of identity.

Treat the required functional content as a stable basis for visual exploration. Candidates may compose it differently without dropping essential information or inventing features. If the user prescribed the identity, explore only the remaining freedom; a small fix needs no identity exercise.

## Find source material with consequences

Look in the brief's actual objects, language, practices, environments, constraints and supplied assets. Identify a few concrete cues that could shape expression. Distinguish supplied evidence from invented associations.

For each useful cue, connect:

> Specific source → relationship worth expressing → visual consequence → usability constraint.

A product category is not a visual rationale. Neither “trustworthy” nor “premium” determines a typeface, palette or layout. Avoid explaining a habitual design after choosing it. A physical reference need not be copied literally; proportion, repetition, sequence, contrast, alignment or annotation may be the useful relationship. Preserve cultural context and avoid stereotypes.

When authorized references would materially help, examine principles rather than copying a brand's recognizable composition, copy or art. Record influential sources and asset provenance. Without reference access, work from available content and label assumptions; do not fabricate research or historical authenticity.

## Make visual alternatives observable

For a substantial fresh identity, create two or three small visual specimens before implementing the full screen. Use the available design medium. In code, a self-contained local `direction-study.html` with comparable candidate sections is enough; no framework or additional service is needed. This is a design-work artifact, not an onboarding step or style picker in the product.

Use the same short sample in each candidate: actual product/title language, one decision-bearing item, a main control and a relevant state. The purpose is to compare expression on functional content, not mood words. A rough but visible specimen is more useful than three paragraphs that all lead to one default treatment.

Give each candidate an organizing visual principle connected to its source material. Explore relationships among:

- **Type composition:** the silhouette, measure, hierarchy, alignment, weight, spacing and interaction of labels, prose and data. Typography is more than swapping a font family.
- **Spatial rhythm:** compactness, intervals, repetition, continuity and the relationship between the whole page and its primary object. Do not let a component library choose this for you.
- **Surface and color:** what is foreground, background, structural boundary and signal. A palette should implement those relationships, not substitute for them.
- **Graphic behavior:** how content is framed, annotated, compared, illustrated or revealed. It should survive beyond a decorative logo or hero image.

The candidates need recognizable differences in the relationships that carry their identity. If neutralizing the accent and hiding the candidate names leaves near-duplicates, develop another direction before choosing. No fixed number of changed properties proves distinctiveness, and novelty must not weaken legibility or a learned control.

Do not make every candidate the same generic utility shell with a different decorative layer. A restrained, conventional direction is a valid candidate when the task supports it; choose it for that reason, not simply because it is easiest to code. Do not equate unusual typography or dense ornament with better design.

## Select from rendered evidence, not a preferred default

Render the specimens before choosing when the environment permits. Inspect the same real text, numerals, main control and state in each. A renderer can supply neutral screenshots without becoming an art director. Without rendering, mark selection provisional and disclose that limitation; a source-based preference is not visual validation.

Do not build a straw-man contest. Give each candidate a plausible use of the same content and comparable care. Do not make one intentionally awkward, overloaded or illegible to justify the familiar option. Repair that weakness or replace the candidate. Rejecting a type composition because it “feels industrial” or a palette because it “feels less warm” is taste, not evidence of poor task fit.

Review these decisions separately before judging the whole:

- **Typography:** compare the actual letterforms, numeral behavior, widths, case, weight and hierarchy on this content. Choose roles from reading and recognition needs. Inspect the available licensed font inventory before settling on the most readily recalled display face; a family name in CSS does not establish that it rendered. Use local fonts or properly licensed assets within the task's resource scope, and retain a deliberate fallback. Do not simulate unavailable fonts or make undeclared network requests. Availability, compactness and visual loudness alone do not establish product specificity.
- **Palette:** establish the relationship between working surface, information, boundaries and signals. Examine value, saturation, coverage and contrast, not merely accent hue. A subject's category does not determine its UI colors. If the rationale is just a category-color association, find a more specific visual premise or use a deliberate neutral foundation. Do not import a familiar background/ink/accent/supporting-surface bundle without examining all its roles.
- **Brand construction:** begin the specimens with the supplied name as text. Develop the product's identity in the working content before designing its mark. Decide the name's recognition role separately from a score, code, headline or other prominent object. Do not automatically copy that object's display family, weight or case into the mark. Case, proportions, alignment, lettering, icon, punctuation suffix and descriptor are independent decisions, not a ready-made logo recipe. Omit additions with no product-specific role. A typographic name can be a complete mark; an icon is not a required deliverable.

For the most tempting candidate, write a short **countercase**: its strongest plausible criticism and the strongest reason to choose another candidate. Use visible evidence. If several directions meet the functional constraints, functionality alone does not select the familiar visual family. Choose the most specific coherent expression, then state the real cost. Do not eliminate a viable expressive direction through an invented usability problem.

If typography or the name treatment remains a habitual choice, make a focused comparison inside the strongest composition before committing. Keep content, geometry and palette constant; give a credible alternative type system equivalent optical care. Compare the actual name, a decision-bearing label and representative data at their intended sizes, including a narrow width. Changing layout and color at the same time can conceal a weak type choice. This is a small extension of the study, not a required extra design phase for every task.

Test the name separately within that comparison. Include the supplied spelling/case as an unforced reference, then explore only changes with a visible reason. Do capitals improve this particular name's shape or recognition, or merely echo the largest object? Does a different family improve the relationship, or simply advertise that a different font was chosen? Preserve the selected direction's character rather than averaging every alternative into safe default typography. Record what the preferred treatment gains and gives up; “bold,” “distinctive” and “fits the category” are not enough to resolve a tie.

When several options remain equally plausible, treat the choice as a design hypothesis instead of inventing a usability win. Revisit the source relationship or accept the uncertainty. Do not replace a recurring display face with a universal serif, force every name into lowercase, rotate through a font list, or add an icon merely to make the outputs count as different. Shared fonts or capitals can be appropriate; the failure is selecting the same relationship by default without examining the content.

Check for a **borrowed bundle**: could this exact font-role pairing, surface palette and brand construction move together to an unrelated product without redesign? If so, change the organizing visual relationship and rework the affected decisions together. Adding a new illustration or swapping the accent is insufficient. This is not a ban on any font, color or mark; a fixed user identity overrides exploration, and individual familiar choices can be justified.

Record a compact identity decision:

> Source relationship → type system → surface/color logic → name/mark construction → strongest rejected alternative and observed tradeoff.

Keep it in the design record, not product copy. Select a coherent direction; do not average the candidates into a common default. Do not ask the user to choose unless a material unresolved preference requires it.

## Preserve the identity during implementation

Build the defining content relationship first, then supporting components. Compare the implemented screen with the selected specimen. Do not let convenient defaults silently replace its type scale, alignment, rhythm or surface relationships.

A logo, tiny illustration or accent panel cannot carry all the identity while the rest of the screen stays interchangeable. Inspect a second meaningful state and a phone layout. The direction should still be recognizable during ordinary work, long text, errors, empty content and reduced motion. Keep primary controls conventional and readable; move expression into composition or content treatment when interaction novelty causes friction.

Recheck typography, palette and mark against the selected study after implementation. Also inspect microcopy, punctuation, decorative arrows, small uppercase labels and framing as a combined signature. Remove unsupported additions instead of replacing them with another universal embellishment. A domain-specific task widget does not excuse a generic identity around it.

## Compare honestly

When previous work is supplied or access is authorized, compare the full grammar: type roles, opening composition, density, surface relationships, accent use, boundaries and graphic behavior. An unrelated product sharing the whole bundle warrants re-examination. A same-product screen generally warrants continuity.

Without history, compare the candidates created for this brief. Do not invent memory, randomize a theme to guarantee difference, or promise uniqueness across unseen projects. A visual study supports a reviewable decision; it does not prove originality or usability with real participants.
